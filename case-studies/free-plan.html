PandaDoc — Free plan

Bringing PandaDoc's free users back after their first document

How I helped make PandaDoc's Free plan worth coming back to, and a starting point to explore PandaDoc.

Role
Senior Product Designer, design owner for Free plan activation and retention
Scope
Product design, UX research, Experiment design, AI Prototyping
Timeline
December 2025 – August 2026

I joined the work on PandaDoc's Free plan in December 2025, working as the designer for both retention and the acquisition team. In 2026 the company made Free its main acquisition channel, with a clear goal: to grow monthly active accounts, and get half of the users who sent a document to send another one the following month. The main problem was that most of them sent a document and never came back. To understand why, we interviewed 17 free users. Then I used what we learned to redesign how people start on Free: onboarding shaped by why they signed up, and a path to Free for customers who cancel.

The challenge

PandaDoc's Free plan was generous, at 60 documents a year, but most people used it once. In 2025, about 15% of free accounts that sent a document sent another the following month.

But the product gave people little reason to come back. New free users had no onboarding at all. Trial sign-ups answered questions about their use case, but free sign-ups skipped them, so everyone got the same upload-and-send path. After the first document was sent, nothing happened. And when paid customers cancelled, Free wasn't offered anywhere, so they left thinking there was no lower tier. In the 1–50 employee segment alone, about 2K paid accounts cancelled every month.

From an inherited roadmap to a retention strategy

When the retention team joined the Free plan work in December 2025, the previous designer handed over the free eSign work. Work on the first send was already paying off: removing template upsells and opening the new editor to free users helped lift 2-day activation of all free sign-ups from 2.3% in October 2025 to 8.3% in January 2026. More people were sending a first document, but most didn't come back, and nobody knew why. From January to March 2026 we ran 17 moderated interviews with 9 active and 8 inactive free users.

Free users were happy overall, with an NPS of 69 in Q2, but they came back rarely.

People weren't leaving because the product failed them. They didn't know what else it could do, and nothing told them at a moment they would notice. Aggressive win-back emails would have missed the point. Guidance had to come at the moment of need and match what each person came for.

The work came together in three areas:

  • Keep accounts in the product: move churned customers to Free instead of losing them.
  • Learn why people signed up: ask a few skippable questions and use the answers to route and personalise.
  • Guide at the right moment: use the welcome screen, the empty home page and the moment after the first send, instead of tutorials people skip.

In July 2026 I proposed the order of the post-activation experiments, and the PM agreed: a personalised post-onboarding screen and the post-first-send moment first, a template nudge built from existing components second, and a ChatGPT and Claude first prompt third, after checking for overlap with another team. Designing the experience

The research and the handover gave me the groundwork: who free users were, where they got stuck and what they never found. [confirm: any journey or lifecycle map you made]

Moving churned customers to Free

Cancelling customers were never offered Free, and the plan comparison screen they saw after expiry didn't show it. I designed the path to Free: a message at the end of the cancellation flow saying the account would move to Free, a banner with a reactivate option until the renewal date, and a dialog introducing Free on the first login after the switch. Lifecycle marketing sent the matching email. [confirm: which pieces you designed]

The first version covered 1–50 employee accounts on Starter, Business and Launch plans, and excluded customers who cancelled over cost or value or by turning off auto-renewal. It reached 100% on 3 March 2026. By mid-April, 739 accounts had moved to Free: 22% were active, 6.6% had sent a document and 9 had reactivated a paid plan. Later, reactivation revenue dropped, and in September the team was discussing whether the routing contributed and whether to roll it back. A parallel test moving expired trials to Free was inconclusive or negative and was paused.

Onboarding that follows intent

Every free user got the same path: upload a document and send it. Unlike trial sign-ups, free users had never been asked why they joined. An earlier test of skippable questions after the first send showed a small lift in month-1 retention. With the acquisition team's PM, I designed intent-based onboarding. A first question asks what the person wants to do (sign something quickly, explore, or integrate via the API), and a second experiment adds connecting ChatGPT or Claude. Each answer leads to its own welcome screen. API sign-ups, for example, can generate their key straight after creating the account.

The flow targeted the two largest free sources, users routed from the trial sign-up and the pricing page, together 61% of free sign-ups. The projection was +9.5 and +6 percentage points in 2-day activation, about 1,000 more activated senders a month. Before launch I wrote a research plan to test whether people understood the options, especially API versus connecting an AI tool, and what they expected after choosing. The experiment was planned for Q4 2026.

Using the moments we already had

Most free users had one or two documents, so their home page looked empty, and nothing happened after their first send. In July 2026 I argued that Free didn't need a new onboarding flow. We needed to use the surfaces we already had, which we could change without cross-team approval or much engineering.

  • A personalised empty state: variants of the post-onboarding screen routed by the pre-onboarding answers. Someone who picked ChatGPT or Claude sees AI tools; someone who picked Google sees Drive and Gmail.
  • The post-first-send moment: a “you sent your first document, here's what's next” screen, triggered when the document is sent.
  • Nudges from existing components: for template nudges I turned down a large new banner and used the tooltips and callouts already available in Amplitude, so we could test copy and timing with no engineering work.

Working prototype-first, I suggested running two cheap experiments side by side instead of one redesign: a new personalised landing page, against the current home page with a better empty state and a product tour. I also argued that the Free pre-onboarding should stay separate from the trial one, so people who upgrade aren't asked the same questions twice. [confirm: what shipped before investment was paused]

Working with AI

The way we worked changed at the same time

In 2026 Growth made a big bet on AI. With AI taking on much of the prototyping and research synthesis, the hard part became deciding what was worth building. From May, teams worked prototype-first, testing ideas as working prototypes before they used up engineering time. Everyone in a team built, whatever their title. My part shifted from drawing screens from scratch to raising the quality of prototypes the whole team made, and judging whether an idea was convincing enough to build.

Outcomes

  • In six months, the team tripled activation and more than doubled month-1 retention, from 14% to 32%. Accounts sending at least one document a month grew by 58%, and monthly active accounts by 42%, partly because churned and trial accounts now landed on Free.
  • With 20% of engineering capacity set aside for improvements, we shipped 14 small UX fixes across the Free plan in Q3 2026.
  • In August 2026 the company adopted a new north star, paid accounts completing 5+ documents a month, and wound down investment in Free.

What I learned

Business strategy moved faster than our experiments. The Free plan went from a top priority in December to a pause in August, and some experiments stopped before they could tell us anything. Small, cheap experiments on surfaces we already owned were the work most likely to teach us something in time, and working prototype-first with AI made them quicker to put in front of people.
