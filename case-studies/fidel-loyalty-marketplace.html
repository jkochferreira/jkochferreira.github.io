Fidel API — Loyalty Marketplace

Making the Loyalty Marketplace easier to navigate

How I redesigned offer discovery in Fidel API's dashboard, for a product that represented 45% of the company's ARR.

Role
Lead Product Designer, Offers team. Sole designer responsible for filtering and sorting, from discovery to design.
Scope
Product design, UX research, usability testing, design systems, prototyping

When I joined Fidel API, I joined the Offers team, which owned the Loyalty Marketplace, the company's main source of revenue. Most loyalty customers used the dashboard rather than the API, so improving that experience was a priority.

I worked on making it easier for customers to find and manage offers, introduced a shared design language as the design team grew, and explored a self-onboarding flow for merchants.

The challenge

The Loyalty Marketplace represented 45% of Fidel API's total ARR. In February and March 2023, it had 37 engaged customers, £1.7M in ARR and 1.9M enrolled cards per month.

The company wanted to improve the customer experience and make its processes easier to manage. Since most loyalty customers worked through the dashboard, that was where we needed to focus.

But the dashboard wasn't keeping up with the growth of the Marketplace. As customers added more brands and offers, finding what they needed became harder. Brands and offers were spread across multiple pages, with endless scrolling and no clear way to narrow down the list. New offers could also appear in unexpected places, making them difficult to find.

From an endless list to a clearer experience

The Marketplace showed all available offers in one long list, with basic information such as reward, channels and programmes. There was no way to filter or sort the results.

Customers didn't need more information on the page. They needed a way to find the offers they were looking for.

I started by exploring filtering, sorting and search, so customers could find offers without scrolling through the whole list. The work also highlighted two other areas that needed attention, and we worked on those too:

Design consistency

Create a shared design language for the dashboard and website as the team grew.

Merchant onboarding

Explore a self-onboarding flow to make it easier for merchants to join loyalty programmes.

Designing the experience

There wasn't much data to help us make design decisions, so I introduced usability testing as a regular part of the process. I used Maze to test prototypes, check assumptions and understand where customers were struggling before we moved into development.

Filtering and sorting

The first version I tested included filters for brand name, segmentation, supplier and country, as well as an "Add filter" option and a separate sorting control.

Based on the feedback, I refined the filters shown by default. The final version included brand, country, content provider and available programmes, while less common options, such as approval, activation and spend requirements, were grouped under "More filters".

Each filter had its own search, so customers working with hundreds of brands could type a name instead of scrolling. Applied filters showed what was selected and how many options were active. Filters could also be combined, with a "Clear all" option to reset them.

I moved sorting into the column headers, making it easier to sort by reward, locations, creation date and programmes. I also designed an empty state for cases where no offers matched the selected filters.

The result worked for different types of customers, from small businesses looking for a single brand to larger teams combining several filters.

Merchant onboarding

Merchants found it difficult to onboard their businesses to loyalty programmes. I explored a self-onboarding flow that would let them identify their business through a small test transaction.

The merchant would receive a link from the programme, use a virtual card to make a charge of $1–$5 on their own terminal, and then confirm the transaction found in the system. This would link their merchant ID to the correct location.

I also designed the different states around the flow, including help finding a merchant ID, rejecting an incorrect match, a transaction that couldn't be found and an expired link.

We tested it as a pilot with a small group of merchants to see whether it would work in their day-to-day operations.

Side project

Shared design language

As the design team grew, it became clear that existing projects had been built without a design system, so each one looked and behaved slightly differently.

I proposed a shared design language for the dashboard and website and built most of it myself, to give customers a more consistent experience and designers and developers a single reference. It included foundations such as colour palettes, typography (Inter and Roboto Mono) and an icon set, as well as core components like buttons, input fields, dropdowns, notifications, and transaction and offer cards.

Another designer worked with me on the guidelines, which explained how to use each element, with do/don't examples and practical rules such as ordering content by importance, validating forms inline, keeping labels consistent and when to use tooltips.

Outcomes

  • Customers could manage their offers more independently, which led to higher conversion among customers using the Marketplace.
  • Conversions expanded into more regions, and customers relied less on the Customer Success team.
  • The shared design language gave the growing design team a consistent base for the dashboard and website.
