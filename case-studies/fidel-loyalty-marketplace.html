When I joined Fidel API, I was assigned to the Offers team, which owned the Loyalty Marketplace, the company's main source of revenue. Most loyalty customers used the platform through the dashboard rather than the API, so the team's goal was to make that experience good enough to attract more customers. I redesigned how customers find offers in the Marketplace, set up a shared design language for the growing design team, and prototyped a self-onboarding flow for merchants.

The challenge

The Loyalty Marketplace represented 45% of Fidel API's total ARR. In February–March 2023 it had 37 engaged customers, £1.7M in ARR and 1.9M enrolled cards per month [confirm: Marketplace or company-wide figures, and OK to publish?].

The company wanted a best-in-class customer experience and simpler supporting processes. With most customers working in the dashboard, that is where the experience had to improve.

The dashboard did not scale with its customers. As a customer worked with more brands, they created more offers, and their frustration grew with it. Because of how the database was set up, brands and offers were spread over many pages and only appeared with endless scrolling. New offers, created or added from the marketplace, landed in a random place in the list, so customers could not find them.

From an endless list to strategy

The Marketplace was a single list of offers with gross reward, channels and programmes, and nothing to narrow it down. [confirm: who designed the original list, and any evidence of the problem beyond this, e.g. support tickets or Customer Success feedback]

Customers didn't need more data in the list; they needed ways to cut it down to what they were looking for. We set out to add filtering, sorting and search, and to fix the things around the feature that slowed the team down.

The work grew into three areas:

  • Find: filtering, sorting and search, so customers could reach any offer without scrolling.
  • Standardise: a shared design language, so new work was consistent and faster to build.
  • Self-serve: a self-onboarding flow for merchants, to reduce the manual work in getting them live.

Designing the experience

There were few metrics to support decisions, so I made usability testing a standard step in our process. I tested prototypes in Maze to check our assumptions before building.

Filtering and sorting

Customers couldn't find their offers in a long, unordered list. The first version I tested had brand name, segmentation, supplier and country filters, an "Add filter" option and a separate sort control. In the final version, the filters shown by default are brand, country, content provider and available programmes. Less common ones (approval, activation, minimum spend to qualify, maximum spend for reward) sit under "More filters". [confirm: what drove the changes between the tested and final versions]

Each filter has its own search, so a customer working with hundreds of brands can type a name instead of scrolling. Applied filters show what is selected and how many ("Brand name: 3"), filters combine, and "Clear all" resets them. Sorting moved into the column headers: reward, locations, created date and programmes. When nothing matches, an empty state explains that no offers fit the criteria.

The result works for a small business owner looking for one brand as well as an enterprise customer combining several filters. In testing, 80% of participants gave it a 10/10 rating and all of them said it would speed up going live with offers.

Merchant onboarding

Merchants found it hard to onboard their businesses to loyalty programmes. I designed a prototype that lets merchants identify themselves with a small test transaction. The merchant gets a link from the programme, uses a virtual card to make a charge of $1–$5 on their own terminal, and confirms the transaction we find. That links their merchant ID to the location. I designed the states around it too: help finding a merchant ID, rejecting a wrong match, a transaction we can't find, and an expired link.

We ran it as a pilot with a small group of merchants to see whether it would work in their day-to-day operations. [confirm: the pilot's outcome and whether it shipped]

A side project: a shared design language

As the design team grew, it became clear that existing projects had been built without a design system. [confirm: who you worked with]

  • Consistency: one experience for customers across the dashboard and website.
  • Shared language: one source for designers and developers.
  • Speed: a faster workflow from design to build.

The system covered the dashboard and the website. It set foundations (colour palettes, a type scale in Inter and Roboto Mono, an icon set), components (buttons, input fields with their anatomy, dropdowns, notifications, transaction and offer cards) and usage guidelines such as do/don't examples, inline form validation, consistent labels and tooltips.
