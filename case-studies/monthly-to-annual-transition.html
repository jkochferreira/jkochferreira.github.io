PandaDoc — Monthly to Annual

From one renewal dialog to a broader annual plan strategy

How a first experiment at PandaDoc led to a new approach to moving monthly customers to annual.

Role
Senior Product Designer, design owner for M2A
Team
PM, 4 engineers, Data, Research and CRM
Services
Product design, experiment design, UX research, design systems
Industry
B2B SaaS, document workflow
Timeline
September 2025 – January 2026

I joined the monthly-to-annual work in September 2025 as the designer on PandaDoc's Monetization squad. An earlier experiment had shown that some customers were willing to switch to annual, but also that a single message wasn't enough. I used those learnings to help define a broader strategy around customer segments, timing and offers. From there, I designed and tested different touchpoints across the customer journey, from plan comparison to trial checkout and customer milestones.

The challenge

Monthly customers churned six times faster than annual customers: 5.18% a month compared with 0.88%. Most of that churn came from teams with 2–50 seats, and only 0.3% of Business Monthly accounts switched to annual each month.

The business goal was to reduce churn in the 2–50 seat segment. Moving monthly accounts to annual could help improve net dollar retention (NDR), but we needed to understand what would make more customers consider the switch.

Annual plans were also not easy to find in the product. The plan comparison page didn't mention them, the link on the billing page had a 0.5% conversion rate, and 60% of trials that started on annual switched to monthly before paying.

From experiment to strategy

The first experiment was designed by another designer. It targeted all Business Monthly admins with a dialog shown once, 14 days before renewal. The dialog highlighted the savings of switching to an annual plan, without offering an extra discount.

The experiment increased monthly-to-annual conversions from 0.3% to 0.98%, reaching our 1% target. But engagement with the dialog was low, with a 1.75% click-through rate. Since a modal interrupts the customer's flow, this suggested that many people were dismissing it without engaging with the offer.

We also collected feedback from 977 customers who declined it. The main reasons were that they preferred the flexibility of monthly plans (30.6%), wanted to switch later (24.3%), or still found the price too high (22.8%).

This made it clear that showing annual savings wasn't enough. We needed to reach the right customers at the right time, with an offer that made sense to them, and give them more than one opportunity to switch.

I designed the strategy around three areas:

Segmentation

Focus on engaged customers, growing teams, power users and accounts at risk of churning.

Timing

Use customer milestones and behaviours, such as team growth or add-on purchases, instead of relying on a single message.

Offers

Explore extra discounts, annual-only perks and bundles based on customer needs.

From this point on, I led the design of the strategy and its touchpoints. I shared my proposals and worked through them with the PM and engineering manager. Together, we decided which ideas to take forward.

Designing the experience

Before designing the new touchpoints, I mapped the customer lifecycle and the communication patterns already available in the product. This helped us decide when to reach customers, which format to use and how much attention each message should ask for.

The lifecycle map covered the first twelve months: activation in months 1–3, adoption in months 4–10, and the periods when customers had historically switched most often, months 1–2 and 12. The idea was to use subtle messages throughout the journey and save more prominent messages for specific milestones.

I also mapped the different ways we could communicate with customers, from tooltips and banners to dialogs and email. This helped clarify the difference between a nudge, which guides without blocking the user's flow, and a modal, which interrupts it.

The work covered several moments in the customer journey, from comparing plans to checkout, billing and key milestones.

Plan comparison

I added a monthly/annual toggle to each plan card to make the savings easier to understand. I also changed the CTAs from "Select plan" to "Upgrade" and "Downgrade".

An earlier pricing study had shown that 3 out of 6 participants struggled to change their billing cycle using a toggle. Before building the new version, we tested the new design in an unmoderated Maze study.

With 30 participants, it showed 93–97% task success, above the 80% benchmark. 67% preferred having the toggle inside each card.

Enterprise plans are annual only. We explored different ways to communicate this, including a greyed-out card and a blurred price. We went with a disabled monthly option and an "Annual only" label. The blur was dropped because it hid the price.

Checkout and billing

I worked on making annual savings easier to spot and understand, using each customer's own numbers.

At checkout, the small savings label was easy to miss, while a larger one competed with the price. I moved the saving inside the plan card instead.

On the billing page, we introduced a more prominent savings component. It received fewer clicks, but a higher share of those clicks converted.

Trial checkout

60% of trials that started on annual switched to monthly before paying. Instead of showing another message to everyone, I designed a tooltip that appeared when a customer selected a monthly plan.

We tested it against a persistent savings card in a 50/50 experiment. The tooltip performed better on paid conversions, new-business MRR and average revenue per account. After 14 days, it delivered a 6.9% increase in paid conversions, 12% in new-business MRR and 12.4% in average revenue per account, compared with the savings card.

The tooltip was then rolled out to all trials.

Customer milestones

A quarter of the customers who declined the first offer said they wanted to switch later. We used that feedback to explore new opportunities around the first and third months.

The third-month campaign combined an email, an in-app dialog and a homepage banner. It offered an extra 20% off Starter annual and 25% off Business annual, with a countdown to make the offer time-limited. We also defined rules for how the messages worked together: dismissing the dialog left the banner visible, while responding to the survey removed the campaign messages.

A side project: a system for nudges

While working on M2A, I noticed that most nudge ideas ended up as either a banner or a modal. We didn't have shared guidance on which pattern to use for each type of message.

Together with another product designer, I reviewed the nudge patterns already in the product and worked on guidelines for how and when to use them.

We had three goals:

Clarify existing patterns

Define which design system components could be used for nudges and when.

Find the gaps

Identify what the existing components couldn't support.

Allow more flexibility

Make it possible to use different patterns for different messages, rather than relying on the same ones every time.

We grouped nudges by what triggered them:

TriggerWhen it happensPatterns
Action-basedAfter a specific user actionTooltip, toast
Signal-basedWhen the user's context suggests they might need helpWidget chat, toast, tooltip
Behaviour-basedAfter a pattern in user behaviour over timeCallout, tooltip, email
Time-basedAt a set time or after a certain periodModal, email
StaticAlways visible to eligible users in a specific placeInline text, hero banner

We also grouped patterns into contextual messages, tied to a specific place in the interface, and non-contextual messages, such as top banners, modals, email and push notifications.

Each pattern got its own guidelines covering when to use it, which triggers fit, how to write the message, and its strengths and risks. The rules were different for each nudge, for example how long a toast stays on screen, or whether a nudge can appear again after being dismissed.

The callout nudge

The biggest gap was a contextual message that could sit between a tooltip and a dialog. Together with the design system team, we considered two options: adding a non-critical variant to the existing Alert component or creating a separate component.

We chose a separate component because alerts are system messages that need attention, while nudges should feel different and shouldn't interrupt the user's work.

The new callout supports an optional icon, short description, highlighted text, button and close action. It has five variants: Default for general recommendations, Highlight for positive messages, Upsell for paid features, AI for AI-powered features, and Reminder for re-engagement. Each variant has an inverted version and comes in small and large widths.

I worked through the final details with the design system team, including the corners, padding and white version with a shadow. The component was added to the design system and was used by the wider team.

Outcomes

  • The trial checkout tooltip performed better than the persistent savings card and was rolled out to all trials. After 14 days, it delivered 6.9% more paid conversions, 12% more new-business MRR and 12.4% higher average revenue per account.
  • Monthly-to-Annual was named the highest-impact churn initiative of FY25. Average churn across the company dropped by about 8% from FY24 to FY25 (year to date). This was a company-wide result, not an impact attributed to my design work alone.
  • The toggle became the standard on the plan comparison page, and the tooltip and the callout became reusable patterns for other teams.

What users said

Feedback from the Maze study on the new plan comparison page (30 participants, October 2025):

“Changing to annual was obvious: one quick toggle.”
“I liked seeing both options without leaving the page.”
“You save money yearly: it's clear right there.”
“It took only a few seconds, everything was clear.”

What I learned

The first experiment was useful not just because of the result, but because of what we learned from it. Low engagement and customer feedback helped us move beyond a single dialog and think about the whole customer journey.

It also reminded me that pushing customers towards annual isn't always the answer. We need to understand what each customer needs and choose the right moment and message.
